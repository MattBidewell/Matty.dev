Every time you type something into a language model, the very first thing
that happens has nothing to do with meaning, grammar, or intent. Your text
gets chopped into pieces — **tokens** — and everything downstream, every
weight, every prediction, only ever operates on those pieces.

A token is not a word. Sometimes it's smaller than one: `"tokenizing"` might
split into `token`, `izing`. Sometimes it's a whole word. Sometimes it's just
a piece of punctuation. The split comes from a vocabulary the model was
trained with, built by statistically finding the chunks that show up often
enough to deserve their own slot.

Watch what happens when the sentence below gets tokenized:

<TokenVisualizer text="the cat sat on the mat" />

Here it's basically one token per word, because every one of those words is
common enough to have its own entry in the vocabulary. Rarer or made-up
words fracture into smaller sub-word pieces instead — which is why a model
can still handle a word it's never seen before: it just assembles it out of
pieces it *has* seen. Same tokenizer, same rules, just a word it hasn't
memorized as a single unit:

<TokenVisualizer text="the flibbertigibbet unbamboozled the pseudoarchaeologist" />

`the` still gets its own single token, both times — it's common enough to
earn that. The three words made up on the spot don't get that privilege:
each one breaks into several pieces instead. That's the real GPT-4o
tokenizer's actual output, not a hand-picked example.

Once the text is tokens, each one gets mapped to a vector — a long list of
numbers — and that's the only form the model ever actually reads. No
letters, no words, just tokens and the vectors they point to.

So the next time a model does something that feels a little too literal, or
splits a number in a way that breaks the arithmetic, remember: it was never
looking at your sentence. It was looking at *this*.
